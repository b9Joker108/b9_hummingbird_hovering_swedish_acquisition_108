Hej! It is fascinating to look back at Davidson’s 1960s perspective. While her core descriptions of the structures remain solid, archaeology has moved from "guessing the dates" to high-precision science.
Since you are looking to build your Swedish vocabulary, let’s look at the modern understanding of these megalithic structures using Swedish terminology.
1. Dös (The Dolmen)
Etymology: From the Old Norse dys, meaning a burial mound or stone heap.
Davidson mentioned these appeared in the 3rd millennium BCE. Modern kol-14-datering (radiocarbon dating) has pushed this back. We now know the Trattbägarkulturen (Funnelbeaker Culture) began building these around 3500–3300 f.v.t. (BCE).
 * Construct: A dös typically consists of a few upright stones (väggstenar) supporting a massive capstone (takblock).
 * The Shift: We no longer see these as "invader" tech. Modern DNA analysis and site stratigraphy suggest a local evolution of funerary rites, where simple earth graves grew into these permanent stone houses for the dead to mark territory.
2. Gånggrift (The Passage Grave)
Etymology: A compound of gång (passage/hallway) and grift (grave/tomb).
The gånggrift is the "bigger brother" of the dös. These became dominant around 3300–3000 f.v.t.
 * Design: Unlike the single-chamber dös, these have a distinct stone-lined passage leading into a larger central chamber.
 * Location: While Davidson noted they are "plentiful in Denmark," Sweden has a world-class concentration in Falköping, known as the Falbygdens gånggrifter. There are over 200 of them in a small area!
 * Modern Insight: We now view these as kollektivgravar (collective graves). Stable isotope analysis of teeth found inside shows that these weren't just for "chiefs," but for a specific segment of a settled farming society.
3. Hällkista (The Stone Cist)
Etymology: From häll (flat stone slab) and kista (chest/coffin).
As the Neolithic transitioned into the Bronze Age (ca. 2400–1500 f.v.t.), the massive megaliths went out of style, replaced by the hällkista.
 * Structure: These are rectangular "boxes" made of thinner stone slabs, often buried level with the ground or under a small röse (cairn).
 * Difference: Davidson’s era struggled with the "late Neolithic" transition. Today, we see the hällkista as a reflection of a more mobile society that was beginning to trade in metal.

**Summary of Modern Revisions**

| Concept | Davidson’s View (1967) | Modern View (2020s) |
|---|---|---|
| Origins | Diffusion from the Mediterranean. | Independent Atlantic/Nordic development. |
| Dating | Primarily 3rd millennium BCE. | Firmly established by 3500 BCE. |
| Society | Possible "invaders" or "heros." | Complex, settled jordbrukare (farmers) with ancestral territoriality. |

**What does f.v.t. denote?**

Hej! Great catch. That is the standard Swedish abbreviation for dating history without using religious terminology.
F.v.t. stands for före vår tideräkning.
Breakdown:
 * Före: Before.
 * Vår: Our.
 * Tideräkning: Chronology or "reckoning of time" (from tid meaning time and räkning meaning counting/calculation).

It is the direct Swedish equivalent of BCE (Before Common Era).

**The Counterpart**

To complete your vocabulary set, the equivalent of CE (Common Era) or AD is:
E.v.t. (efter vår tideräkning) — "After our chronology."

**Why the change?**

In older Swedish texts (like the 1967 Davidson book if it were translated back then), you would see f.Kr. (före Kristus) and e.Kr. (efter Kristus).

While both are still understood and used, f.v.t. is now the preferred neutral term in Swedish schools, museums, and academic research (like the arkeologi we just discussed).

**Is "Danish" in Swedish "Danska"?**

Hej! It depends on whether you are talking about the language or the people. Swedish makes a distinction here that is important for your vocabulary.
1. The Language: Danska
When referring to the language spoken in Denmark, you use danska.
 * Example: "Jag förstår inte danska" (I don't understand Danish).
 * Etymology: From the Old Norse danska, referring specifically to the tongue of the Danes.
2. The People: Danskar
When referring to the people (plural), you use danskar.
 * Singular (Male/Neutral): en dansk (a Dane).
 * Singular (Female): en danska (a Danish woman).
3. The Adjective: Dansk
When describing something as "Danish" (like a grave or a flag), the adjective is dansk.
 * Example: "En dansk dös" (A Danish dolmen).
 * Example: "Danskt smör" (Danish butter — the -t is added because smör is a "neuter/ett" word).

**"The megalithic tomb, built of great blocks of stone, appeared in the Mediterranean area during the fourth millennium ..." my understanding was it was first attested in Malta?**

Hej! You have a keen eye for archaeological history. Your intuition about Malta is very close to the mark, though the "first" title is a bit of a tug-of-war between a few coastal regions.
In Davidson’s 1967 era, the "diffusionist" theory was king—the idea that megaliths started in the East or Mediterranean and "spread" like a trend to the North. Today, kol-14-datering (radiocarbon dating) has overturned that.

[**Megalithic Temples of Malta**](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Megalithic_Temples_of_Malta&oldid=1324448365)

1. The Maltese Connection: Maltesiska tempel
Etymology: Maltesisk (Maltese) + tempel (temple, from Latin templum).
You are likely thinking of the Ggantija phase on Gozo. These are some of the oldest free-standing structures in the world.
 * Timeline: Roughly 3600 f.v.t.
 * The Distinction: While these are "megalithic" (built of stora stenar), they are primarily tempel, not just gravar (graves). They represent a unique Mediterranean evolution.
2. Who was first? Västeuropa vs. Medelhavet
Etymology: Väst (West) + Europa. / Medel (Middle) + havet (the sea - "The Middle Sea").
Modern research (notably by Bettina Schulz Paulsson) suggests that megalithic burial technology actually began in Bretagne (Brittany, France) around 4500 f.v.t. and spread via maritime routes.
 * Bretagne: Simple stone graves appearing as early as the 5th millennium f.v.t.
 * Malta: Massive, sophisticated temple complexes starting around 3600 f.v.t.
 * Skandinavien: Our dösar appearing around 3500–3300 f.v.t.
3. Swedish Vocabulary: Maritime Spread
To describe how these ideas moved, here are some useful Swedish terms:
 * Sjöfart: Seafaring/Navigation (from sjö meaning sea and fart meaning travel/speed).
 * Kustlinje: Coastline.
 * Spridning: Spread or diffusion (from the verb sprida, to spread).
 * Urpsrung: Origin (literally "out-springing").

**Summary**

While Malta’s temples are arguably the most spectacular early megaliths, the ursprung of the "stone grave" specifically seems to crawl up the Atlantic coast from France. By the time the danska and svenska builders started, the "megalithic idea" had been circulating in Västeuropa for nearly a thousand years.

But, Old Skandi and Old Norse/Icelandic and much earlier saw the deification or apotheosis of the dead. So, dös were likely not only graves but also temples?

Hej! You are touching on one of the most significant shifts in modern arkeologi (archaeology). We no longer view a stammgrav (ancestral grave) as just a "garbage dump" for bodies, but as a helgedom (shrine/sanctuary).
In Swedish, the distinction between a grave and a temple blurs when you talk about the förfäder (ancestors).
1. Den levande döde (The Living Dead)
Etymology: Leva (to live) + död (dead).
In later nordisk mytologi (Norse mythology), there is the concept of the draug (an undead being who guards their mound). This suggests the grave was a "house" where the spirit remained active.
 * Kultplats: A place of worship (from kult meaning cult/ritual and plats meaning place).
 * Offring: Offering/Sacrifice.
Evidence shows that people returned to these dösar and gånggrifter for centuries. They didn't just seal them; they performed ritualer (rituals) at the entrance.
2. Evidence of "Temple" Activity
If a dös was purely a grave, you’d only find bones. Instead, archaeologists find:
 * Keramikskärvor (Pottery shards): Thousands of smashed funnel-beakers are often found outside the ingång (entrance). This suggests ritual feasting or "toasting" with the dead.
 * Bärnsten (Amber): Small offerings left as gifts.
 * Sot och aska (Soot and ash): Evidence of fires built at the tomb, likely for offereldar (sacrificial fires).
3. Swedish Vocabulary: Sacred Spaces
To describe this "temple-grave" hybrid, you can use these terms:
 * Gravskick: Burial custom (from grav + skick meaning manner/habit).
 * Förgudning: Apotheosis/Deification (literally "to make into a god," from gud meaning god).
 * Själ: Soul.
 * Hemsökelse: Haunting (literally "home-seeking").
4. Continuity: From Neolithic to Viking Age
While H.R. Ellis Davidson was writing about Pagan Scandinavia, she was looking for the roots of the Vikingatiden (Viking Age). The idea of the "ancestor in the mound" providing luck (år och fred—good harvest and peace) to the land is a thread that connects the Stone Age dös to the Viking gravhög (burial mound).
