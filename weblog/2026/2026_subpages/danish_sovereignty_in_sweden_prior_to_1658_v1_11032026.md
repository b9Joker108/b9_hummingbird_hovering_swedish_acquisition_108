Danish Sovereignty in Sweden (pre-1658)

Before 1658, the “Danish sovereignty in Sweden” question is really about which parts of what is now southern and western Sweden were under the Danish (or Danish‑Norwegian) crown, and how that overlapped with a changing Swedish realm.

## Key regions under Danish rule

The core Danish possessions later transferred to Sweden were the so‑called Skåneland provinces and Bohuslän; Trøndelag (now Norway) and Bornholm were also briefly lost and then recovered.

- **Skåneland (the Scanian provinces)**:  
  - Scania (Skåne)  
  - Blekinge  
  - Halland  
  These were long‑standing parts of the Danish kingdom, culturally and ecclesiastically oriented toward Copenhagen and Lund, not Stockholm, until the mid‑17th century.[1][3][9]

- **Bohuslän**:  
  - Medievally part of the Norwegian kingdom but under the same Danish‑Norwegian monarch, it bordered Swedish Västergötland and served as Denmark‑Norway’s outlet on the Bohus fortress and the Norwegian west coast.[7][9]

- **Later war gains and losses (1645–1660)**:  
  - Halland was effectively transferred to Sweden for 30 years in the Treaty of Brömsebro (1645), before becoming permanently Swedish in 1658.[7][9]
  - The Treaty of Roskilde (1658) then forced Denmark‑Norway to cede Scania, Blekinge, Halland, Bohuslän, Bornholm, and Trøndelag to Sweden.[5][9][10]
  - The Treaty of Copenhagen (1660) confirmed Swedish rule over Scania, Blekinge, Halland, and Bohuslän but returned Bornholm to Denmark and Trøndelag to Norway, fixing essentially the modern frontier.[1][3][8][10]

So in territorial terms, “Danish sovereignty in Sweden (pre‑1658)” refers mainly to the fact that all of present‑day Skåne, Blekinge, and Halland, plus the coast of Bohuslän, were not Swedish at all but integrated Danish (or Danish‑Norwegian) lands until the mid‑seventeenth century.[1][3][9][10]

## Political and legal status before 1658

- These provinces were not colonial possessions; they were integral parts of the Danish kingdom (or Denmark‑Norway), with their own provincial laws but under Danish royal authority, the Danish Council of the Realm, and the Danish church hierarchy. Lund, for example, was a key archiepiscopal and intellectual center in Denmark.[1]
- Local elites—nobility, clergy, and peasantry—were tied into the Danish political system and diets, not the Swedish Riksdag.[1]
- Even after the Swedish conquest, Sweden initially guaranteed the continuation of local law and privileges (e.g., the Malmö Recess of 1662 kept Scanian laws and representation), which shows how distinct these regions still were.[1]

## Strategic and dynastic context

- Control over both shores of the Öresund (Sound) was central to Danish power; holding Skåne, Blekinge, Halland, and Bohuslän gave Denmark‑Norway command of key sea lanes and tolls.[3][5]
- The long Dano‑Swedish rivalry (with multiple wars in the 16th–17th centuries) is essentially about Sweden breaking out of this Danish strategic ring and turning the Baltic into a Swedish‑dominated sea, culminating in the “march across the Belts” and the harsh Roskilde terms.[5][7][9][10]

## Timeline sketch (very compressed)

- Medieval period–early 17th c.: Skåne, Blekinge, Halland firmly Danish; Bohuslän Norwegian under a shared Danish‑Norwegian crown. Sweden is still largely confined to the eastern side of the peninsula.[1][3][9]
- 1645 (Brömsebro): Sweden gains Halland (initially temporarily), plus other territories, weakening Danish control of the west coast.[7][9]
- 1657–1658: Danish‑Swedish war; Charles X Gustav’s “march across the Belts” forces Denmark into the Treaty of Roskilde.[5][7][9]
- 1658 (Roskilde): Denmark‑Norway cedes Skåne, Blekinge, Halland, Bohuslän, Bornholm, Trøndelag; these “east of the Sound” lands become Swedish on paper.[1][5][9][10]
- 1658–1660: Renewed war; siege of Copenhagen; external powers intervene.[5][8]
- 1660 (Copenhagen): Sweden keeps Skåne, Blekinge, Halland, Bohuslän; Denmark retains Bornholm, Norway retains Trøndelag, locking in the enduring border.[1][3][8][10]
